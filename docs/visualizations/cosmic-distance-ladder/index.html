CHOOSE YOUR DEPTH

1 · Discover and play

Hold one finger in front of your nose. Close one eye, then the other. Your finger seems to jump sideways! Nearby stars also seem to move against faraway stars when Earth moves around the Sun. The farther away a star is, the smaller the jump.

Try it: choose Parallax and move the distance slider. Predict what happens to the star's apparent shift. Then try Brightness: how much dimmer does a light become as you move it away?

For very distant galaxies, astronomers use special kinds of stars and exploding stars as cosmic measuring tools.

2 · Explore and understand

A parsec is the distance at which one astronomical unit subtends one arcsecond, approximately 3.26 light-years. Stellar parallax uses the half-angle between opposite positions in Earth's orbit: d(pc) = 1/p(arcsec).

Apparent brightness is not intrinsic luminosity. For an isotropic source in transparent Euclidean space, flux F = L/(4πd²). Doubling distance reduces flux to one quarter.

Classical Cepheids pulsate: their period predicts their average absolute magnitude after calibration. Standardized Type Ia supernovae reach greater distances. At cosmological scales, observed redshift is interpreted with a model of expansion.

Experiment: change Cepheid period and apparent magnitude; compare the derived distance. Investigate why extinction makes objects appear farther away if ignored.

3 · Analyze and model

For small angles, p(rad) ≈ 1 AU/d; by definition d(pc) = 1/p(arcsec). A measured parallax with uncertainty σp has approximate fractional distance uncertainty σd/d ≈ σp/p only when σp/p is small. Inverting noisy or negative parallaxes requires a probabilistic treatment.

The distance modulus is μ = m − M − A = 5 log₁₀(d/10 pc), where A is extinction in the observed band. Thus d(pc) = 10^((m−M−A+5)/5). The lab uses a clearly labeled illustrative classical-Cepheid calibration M = −2.76(log₁₀ P − 1) − 4.05, with P in days; real work must account for passband, metallicity, and calibration.

Type Ia supernovae are standardized through their light-curve shape and colour. The simplified lab uses an assumed standardized M = −19.3 mag and an adjustable extinction term, not an empirical light-curve fitter.

At low redshift z ≪ 1, d ≈ cz/H₀. The graph is restricted to low z; a cosmological luminosity-distance integral is needed at larger redshift. CSV exports contain model-generated values, never telescope measurements.

4 · Investigate and research

The ladder is a calibration network, not a unique mandatory sequence. Geometric anchors (parallax, eclipsing binaries, masers) calibrate standard candles; overlapping independent methods test systematic errors. Important effects include Gaia parallax zero-point and spatial correlations, crowding and blending, Cepheid metallicity and reddening, selection/Malmquist bias, photometric zero points, and covariance across anchors.

For spatially flat FLRW cosmology, luminosity distance is dL(z) = (1+z)c/H₀ ∫₀ᶻ dz′/E(z′), where E(z) = H(z)/H₀. In flat ΛCDM, E(z) = √[Ωm(1+z)³ + Ωr(1+z)⁴ + ΩΛ]. The local linear Hubble model in this page is only its low-redshift approximation. Distinguish luminosity, angular-diameter and comoving distances.

For uncertainty in a modulus, σd/d ≈ (ln 10/5)σμ. Correlated calibration uncertainties cannot be reduced as if every object were statistically independent. The Hubble tension concerns discrepancies among inference pathways; this demonstration does not resolve it.

Research challenge: compare the impact of a shared zero-point offset with independent random errors, and explain why adding more objects cannot eliminate a common systematic bias.
