Understand what you see

Level 1 · Discover and play

Think of dropping pebbles onto a beach. Each pebble lands in one place. Electrons also make separate little marks when detected. But unlike ordinary pebbles, many electrons passing through two tiny openings make a striped pattern.

We cannot know the exact place the next mark will appear. Quantum physics predicts which places are more likely. Watch the dots build up. The stripes are not painted onto the screen: they appear after many separate detections.

Try it: Fire one, then ten, then a thousand. Close one opening. Are the stripes still there?

Seeing an electron as a dot does not mean it traveled like a tiny marble with a known path. The experiment tells us where it was detected, not a classical route through the apparatus.

Level 2 · Explore and understand

In 1924, Louis de Broglie proposed that matter has a wavelength. An electron accelerated by a voltage U has momentum p and, at modest energies, wavelength λ = h/p ≈ h/√(2mₑeU). Increasing voltage increases momentum, so the wavelength becomes shorter.

The probability amplitudes associated with the two slits combine. They reinforce at some screen positions and oppose at others. A detection still occurs as one localized event, but repeated events are distributed according to the squared magnitude of the combined amplitude.

Near the middle of the screen, neighboring fringe maxima are separated by approximately Δx ≈ λL/d, where L is screen distance and d the separation of slit centers. Increase d: fringes move closer together. Increase U: λ shrinks, so the fringes also move closer together.

Test a prediction: Keep the other settings fixed and double the voltage. Does the fringe spacing halve, or decrease by a factor of about √2? Verify with the numerical readout.

Having a measurement system that can perfectly distinguish the two paths destroys their coherent interference term. Conscious observation is not required: interaction with a path marker suffices.

Level 3 · Analyze and model

Assume a coherent, monochromatic paraxial electron beam, two identical uniformly transmitting rectangular slits of width a and center separation d, and a sufficiently distant screen. In the Fraunhofer approximation, the one-slit probability density is proportional to sinc²(πa sinθ/λ). For two equally weighted, coherent slits:

P₂(x) ∝ sinc²(β) cos²(α),   β = πa sinθ/λ,   α = πd sinθ/λ.

Here sinc(z) = sin(z)/z with sinc(0) = 1, and sinθ = x/√(L² + x²). The familiar cos² expression is proportional to [1 + cos(2α)]/2; this is the form computed by the simulator. The density is numerically normalized on a finite detector window of ±180 µm, so the graph does not represent absolute beam throughput.

The detector blur control convolves the ideal transverse density with a Gaussian point-spread function of standard deviation σ, truncated and renormalized at the window edges. Individual impacts are sampled from the same blurred distribution plotted in blue. A histogram of N samples fluctuates around it and converges statistically as N grows.

For acceleration from rest, p ≈ √(2mₑeU) assumes negligible relativistic corrections, while λ = h/p remains valid more generally. The geometry uses an ideal free-space model and omits electron-optical magnification, aperture charging, and source coherence limitations.

Quantitative experiment: Change slit width a. Predict the first zero of the diffraction envelope at approximately |x| ≈ λL/a, then inspect how the outer fringes disappear.

Level 4 · Investigate and research

For equal-amplitude coherent paths the post-aperture state can be represented schematically as (|1⟩ + |2⟩)/√2. If the paths become entangled with a marker, the joint state takes the form (|1⟩|m₁⟩ + |2⟩|m₂⟩)/√2. Tracing out an unread marker reduces the off-diagonal coherence by the overlap γ = |⟨m₁|m₂⟩|.

For a pure, ideal, equally populated two-path marker model, the path distinguishability D and coherence obey γ = √(1 − D²). The program uses the envelope-weighted expression

P(x) ∝ sinc²(β) [1 + γ cos(2α)] / 2,   γ = √(1 − D²).

Before finite-resolution blur, the local two-beam visibility in this ideal equal-intensity case is V = γ. The general visibility–distinguishability bound is V² + D² ≤ 1 (Englert, 1996). Finite slit width, unequal beam strengths, averaging, decoherence and detector resolution can reduce measured visibility further. The slider models an ideal path marker; it does not calculate a physical microscopic detector interaction.

More rigorous treatments propagate an initial wave packet through a potential using the time-dependent Schrödinger equation or a propagator, account for source transverse coherence and energy spread, and compute the instrument response. A charged electron interacting with material edges may acquire extra phases. This page instead uses a transparent, stationary Fraunhofer probability model.

Historical apparatus distinction: Merli, Missiroli and Pozzi used an electron biprism, not two machined openings. A biased fine filament between grounded plates deflects two coherent portions of an electron wave toward an overlap region; the detection pattern then builds up one event at a time. Jönsson's earlier slit experiment (1961), the 1976 Bologna result, Tonomura's 1989 biprism result and Bach and colleagues' 2013 controlled physical double-slit experiment answer related but technically distinct questions.

Research question: Keep both slits open. Increase D toward 1. Why is the broad diffraction envelope retained while the fine interference fringes disappear? Compare this to increasing detector blur: the graphs can look similar, but the underlying physical mechanisms differ.

The observations do not uniquely select Copenhagen, Bohmian, many-worlds or another empirically equivalent interpretation. “An electron literally took both paths” is interpretation-dependent language; the experimentally supported statement concerns interfering amplitudes and their detection probabilities.

From de Broglie's proposal to electrons arriving one at a time

1924

Louis de Broglie proposes a wavelength for matter: λ = h/p.

1927

Davisson–Germer and G. P. Thomson establish electron diffraction experimentally.

1961

Claus Jönsson publishes electron interference through fabricated fine slits.

1976

Pier Giorgio Merli, Gian Franco Missiroli and Giulio Pozzi publish their electron-biprism single-detection buildup (submitted in 1974).

1989

Akira Tonomura and colleagues report a later influential single-electron buildup movie using an electron biprism and sensitive counting techniques.

2013

Roger Bach and colleagues demonstrate controlled electron diffraction using physical nanofabricated double slits.

Guided experiments

  1. Statistical buildup: compare 1, 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 impacts against the theoretical curve.
  2. One versus two slits: keep everything else fixed and watch the fine fringe modulation disappear when only one slit is open.
  3. Energy and wavelength: at Level 2, increase accelerating voltage from 100 V to 400 V. The nonrelativistic wavelength, and fringe spacing, should approximately halve.
  4. Geometry and diffraction: at Level 3, increase slit separation to compress fringes; increase slit width to narrow the broad envelope.
  5. Coherence versus resolution: at Level 4, compare increasing D to blurring the detector. Both suppress visible fringes, but for different reasons.

Assumptions and limitations

This is a synthetic calculation, not historical footage or empirical observations. It assumes nonrelativistic electrons, a coherent monochromatic source, two ideal equal rectangular apertures, a paraxial far-field approximation and a detector spanning ±180 µm in one transverse coordinate. Some extreme control combinations may no longer strictly satisfy the Fraunhofer condition; a Fresnel or full wave-packet model would then be more accurate.

Samples are independent draws from a normalized one-dimensional probability density. The other on-screen direction is randomized only to make separate events visible. Physical throughput, energy spread, Coulomb repulsion, environmental decoherence, finite source size and detector efficiency are not simulated. The path-distinguishability slider is a specific idealized equal-path coherence model, not a general quantum-measurement simulator.
