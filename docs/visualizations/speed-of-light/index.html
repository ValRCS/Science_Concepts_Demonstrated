How fast does light travel?

Light is extraordinarily fast—but not instantaneous. In 1676, Danish astronomer Ole Rømer used eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io as a cosmic clock and discovered that light takes time to reach us.

THE 1676 BREAKTHROUGH

How an eclipse answered an ancient question

  1. Io acts like a clock. Its regular eclipses provide repeatable events that astronomers can predict.
  2. Earth moves. Over months, Earth changes its distance from Jupiter. Eclipses appear progressively later when Earth moves away, earlier when it approaches.
  3. Rømer tests a prediction. He warned the Paris Academy that the eclipse expected on 9 November 1676 would be about 10 minutes late—and observations supported him.
  4. Light takes time. Rømer estimated roughly 22 minutes for light to cross the diameter of Earth's orbit. The modern value for a 2 AU baseline is about 16 minutes 38 seconds.
RØMER'S HISTORICAL INFERENCE≈ 22 minAcross an orbital diameter; historical estimate
MODERN CALCULATION≈ 16 min 38 sAcross exactly 2 AU at the defined SI value of c

Important historical distinction: Rømer showed that light has a finite propagation time. He did not directly report the modern numerical speed in km/s; translating his time estimate into a speed requires an assumed astronomical unit. Christiaan Huygens later developed a numerical estimate. Earlier thinkers had already suggested that light could have a finite speed.

Discover and play roughly ages 7–10

Imagine you are watching a friend clap from very far away. You see the clap before you hear it, because sound is slower than light. But even light needs a tiny amount of time to travel!

Now think about Io, a moon of giant Jupiter. It disappears into Jupiter's shadow again and again. It's like a giant clock in space. When Earth is far away, we see that clock's tick later—even though the tick itself did not change.

Try this: Set Earth to 0° and read the travel time. Move it to 180°. Does the light have a longer trip? Choose “Light arrives instantly.” What happens to the orange comparison line?

Remember: what we see now may have happened a little while ago. Sunlight reaching us left the Sun about eight minutes earlier.

Explore and understand roughly ages 14–18

Speed tells us how far something travels in a given time: speed = distance ÷ time. In a vacuum, light moves at 299,792,458 metres per second. This value is conventionally written as c. Light travels more slowly through materials such as water or glass; c refers to vacuum.

Io circles Jupiter in approximately 1.769 days. Eclipses form a repeatable clock, but the arrival time of light depends on distance. Over months, the extra seconds between successive observed eclipses accumulate into a difference of minutes. Near and far positions along Earth's orbit differ in distance to Jupiter by approximately 2 astronomical units (AU) in our simplified geometry.

travel time = distance / c

One AU is exactly 149,597,870,700 m. The extra time across 2 AU is approximately 998 seconds = 16 min 38 s. Rømer's historical estimate of 22 minutes was longer, but his observation that the time was not zero was revolutionary.

Try this: Choose the “Rømer's historical” comparison. At 180°, how far apart are the two model curves? Why is the estimated speed different? Then calculate the Moon's one-way light-time in the calculator.

GPS, radar ranging, spacecraft communications, and astronomy all depend on finite signal propagation times. A light-year is a unit of distance, not time: the distance light covers in a Julian year.

Analyze and model bachelor level

Set Jupiter at the fixed position (R, 0) and Earth on a coplanar circular orbit of radius a = 1 AU. The model holds Jupiter's heliocentric distance at R = 5.2 AU, neglects Jupiter's orbital motion, treats Io's position relative to Jupiter as negligible, and uses Euclidean distances:

D(θ) = √[R² + a² − 2Ra cos(θ)]

For a constant vacuum propagation speed c, the light-travel time is τ(θ) = D(θ) / c. The plotted residual relative to the minimum distance is:

Δτ(θ) = [D(θ) − (R − a)] / c

Thus the largest residual is Δτ(π) = 2a/c, independent of R as long as R > a. Because the astronomical unit and c have exact SI values, this idealized difference is approximately 998.009 seconds. The “instantaneous” comparison sets propagation delay to zero; the historical comparison replaces c by the speed cR = 2 AU / (22 min) implied retrospectively by Rømer's time estimate using today's AU.

Let successive eclipse events occur at idealized emission times tn = t0 + nP, where P ≈ 1.769137786 days. An approximate observer's arrival time is Tn ≈ tn + D[θ(tn)]/c. Therefore:

Tn+1 − Tn ≈ P + {D[θ(tn + P)] − D[θ(tn)]}/c

The “one Io clock interval” readout shows that last correction in seconds, with Earth moving 360° × P / 365.25 days between events. It is not the same quantity as the cumulative delay displayed in the graph.

Investigate and research master level

Forward model and observability. A more faithful reconstruction should solve a light-time equation for each observed Io eclipse. For emission at te and reception at to, to − te = |rE(to) − rIo(te)|/c, with positions taken from an ephemeris. The model on this page instead evaluates a static Earth–Jupiter separation at an orbital phase. It ignores light-time iteration, planetary eccentricities and inclinations, Io's orbital perturbations, changes in the Sun–Jupiter distance, eclipse geometry and visibility, observer location, and instrumental timing uncertainty.

Inference. With measured eclipse residuals yi and an ephemeris-based baseline Di, a model might be yi = α + β[Di − Dref] + εi, where β = 1/c, α is an offset and εi includes clock and model errors. Correlated errors and ephemeris uncertainties matter; unknown orbital dynamics can be partially degenerate with a propagation delay. The present graph does not fit any empirical eclipse timings or estimate a confidence interval.

Historical interpretation. Giovanni Domenico Cassini recognized timing irregularities, while Rømer defended a finite-propagation interpretation and made a falsifiable eclipse prediction in 1676. His 22-minute figure is a historical light-time inference, not an SI-calibrated direct measurement of 299,792,458 m/s. Huygens later used such timing information to infer a numerical speed. Bradley's 1728 stellar-aberration work supplied an independent method; later laboratory measurements and electromagnetic theory expanded the evidence base.

Contemporary physics. In special relativity c is an invariant limiting speed for causal signals locally in vacuum, not merely a measured property of a particular light source. Since 1983 the metre has been defined through a fixed numerical value of c (retained in the revised SI). Its numerical exactness in m/s is therefore definitional; experiments test physical theories and realize length/time standards, rather than repeatedly assigning uncertainty to that defined number. Wave phase, group velocity, signal front and propagation in dispersive media require separate treatment.

Research challenge: What bias would result if one tried to fit 1/c while assuming Jupiter remains fixed for a full Earth year? Formulate a residual model with an unknown ephemeris error and explain how independent timing or astrometric observations might break that degeneracy.
