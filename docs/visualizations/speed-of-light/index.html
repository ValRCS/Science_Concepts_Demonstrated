PHYSICS · LIGHT · ASTRONOMY

How fast does light travel?

Light is astonishingly fast, but its journey takes time. Discover how the changing distance between Earth and Jupiter helped astronomers find this out in 1676.

EXPLAIN WHAT YOU OBSERVED

The science behind the experiment

1 · Discover and play

Imagine seeing your friend clap from across a giant field. You see the clap before you hear it: sound needs time to travel. Light travels much faster than sound, but even light takes time.

Jupiter has a moon called Io. Sometimes Io disappears into Jupiter's shadow. That happens at Jupiter first. Earth sees the change only when light has finished the trip to us. A longer trip means a later view.

Try: Click Near Jupiter, then Send the eclipse signal. Next try Far away. Which trip takes longer? Why does the eclipse itself not have to happen any later?

That is what astronomers learned: far-away things show us light from the past. Sunlight reaching Earth began its journey about eight minutes earlier.

2 · Explore and understand

In 1676, astronomer Ole Rømer studied repeated appearances and disappearances of Jupiter's moon Io. When Earth moved away from Jupiter, observed events became progressively later; when Earth approached, they became earlier. A small extra delay per event accumulates over many events.

Light's vacuum speed is 299,792,458 m/s, written c. Travel time is t = d/c. One astronomical unit (AU) is exactly 149,597,870,700 m. In our simplified geometry, the difference between the near and far journeys is 2 AU, corresponding to about 16 min 38 s of extra light time.

Rømer proposed that light needed roughly 22 minutes to cross the diameter of Earth's orbit—an imperfect numerical estimate, but evidence that the delay was not zero. His prediction that an Io event in November 1676 would appear roughly ten minutes late matched observations. That specific prediction is a historical comparison with earlier astronomical tables, not the 16-minute near–far difference in our diagram.

Try: Move Earth from 0° to 180° and read the travel time. Change the comparison from instantaneous light to Rømer's estimate. Then use the light-travel calculator to find the signal time between Earth and the Moon.

Real astronomical distances change. Light in glass or water has different propagation characteristics; c always means the speed of light in vacuum.

3 · Analyze and model

Let Earth travel on a coplanar circle of radius a = 1 AU about the Sun. Fix Jupiter at (R,0), where R > a. The Earth–Jupiter separation follows the cosine law: D(θ)=√(R²+a²−2Ra cos θ). Travel time is τ(θ)=D(θ)/c after converting AU to kilometres.

The cumulative arrival-time residual relative to θ=0 is Δτ(θ)=[D(θ)−(R−a)]/c. Its maximum at θ=180° is 2a/c, independent of R for this geometry. This residual is not itself the extra delay between consecutive Io eclipses.

For a clock with emission period P, with Earth moving 360°P/Y between ticks (Y=365.25 days), the extra observed interval is approximately δP(θ)=[D(θ+360°P/Y)−D(θ)]/c. Its sign changes as Earth moves from receding to approaching Jupiter. At the farthest point, the cumulative residual is largest even though the adjacent-interval correction is close to zero.

Use the model sliders to adjust R and P. Change the graph quantity between cumulative difference in minutes and per-eclipse interval correction in seconds. The hypothetical-speed comparison is a deliberately counterfactual test, not a change to physical c. The exported CSV contains model predictions only.

Try: Increase R while keeping Earth's orbital radius at 1 AU. Why does the maximum cumulative delay stay approximately constant, while the shape of the interval-correction curve changes? Use the two graphs to explain.

4 · Investigate and research

A realistic inversion needs retarded-time geometry. For an emission event at te and observation at to, solve to−te = |rE(to)−rIo(te)|/c using accurate ephemerides. The simplified model neglects Jupiter's motion, orbital eccentricity and inclination, Io's position and perturbations, eclipse visibility, the observer's location, atmospheric and instrumental effects, and light-time iteration.

In the synthetic inversion, let xi be predicted extra light-time calculated at modern c for each orbital phase. Generate yi = α+(1+b)xi+εi, with α a fixed clock offset, b a deliberately hidden distance-scale bias and ε independent Gaussian timing noise. Fit y = A + kx by ordinary least squares; the recovered speed is ĉ = c/k. An instantaneous-light model uses a free constant only, A = mean(y).

The ordinary least-squares variance of the slope is approximately s² / Σ(xi−x̄)², with s² = SSE/(n−2). Propagate it to the speed using σc ≈ c σk/k². This is statistical uncertainty conditional on the chosen model, not total uncertainty. A systematic distance-scale error biases k and c even with many data points; random noise alone does not explain every possible discrepancy.

Historically, Giovanni Domenico Cassini also studied Io's timing irregularities and considered finite light propagation. Rømer developed a particularly persuasive argument and prediction in 1676; subsequent historical scholarship examines their respective roles. Rømer did not directly measure the modern SI value of c, which is now exact by definition under the SI metre convention. Neither our circular-orbit simulation nor its generated data is a reconstruction of the Paris Observatory's 1676 observations.

Try: Set noise to zero and distance bias to +10%. Does the fit give exactly the defined vacuum speed? Now add noise and increase sample count. Which error decreases, and which systematic effect remains? Explain the parameter degeneracy.

THE DISCOVERY · 1676

A cosmic clock changed astronomy

1

Predict the clock

Io's recurring eclipses make a repeated event astronomers can compare across months.

2

Notice a pattern

As Earth recedes, arrival times become later; as it approaches, they become earlier.

3

Test an explanation

Rømer publicly predicted a roughly ten-minute delay for an event in November 1676. It was observed.

4

Infer finite light-time

He inferred about 22 minutes to cross Earth's orbital diameter; the modern figure is about 16 min 38 s.

This episode involved a wider astronomical community, including Cassini and Picard. The historical prediction concerned an event compared with an earlier timing forecast; it must not be confused with our full near–far geometric maximum.
