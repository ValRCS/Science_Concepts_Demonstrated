EARTH & SPACE / GEOCHRONOLOGY / NUCLEAR PHYSICS

How old is it? Ask the atoms.

Some atoms transform naturally. Their individual changes are unpredictable, but in a large sample the proportion remaining follows a reliable pattern. Scientists use that pattern—together with the history of the material—to estimate ages.

Atoms can tell us about long-ago events

Imagine finding a piece of wood from an ancient fire. While the tree was alive, it exchanged carbon with its surroundings. After it died, its radioactive carbon-14 gradually diminished. If we know what to compare it with, we can estimate how long ago the tree stopped exchanging carbon.

Each radioactive atom might change at any moment. We cannot predict which one changes next. But if we have a huge number of them, we can predict how many will remain on average.

  1. In the main experiment, start at zero half-lives. Most dots are still their original color.
  2. Move to one half-life. About half should still represent parent atoms.
  3. Move to two half-lives. Ask: half of the original atoms, or half of the ones that remained?

Remember: one half-life removes about half of what is still there—not the same number of atoms every time.

Understanding half-life and choosing the right clock

An isotope is a form of an element with a particular number of neutrons. A radioactive parent nuclide transforms through nuclear decay. Its half-life is the time required for half of a large parent population to decay on average. A daughter nuclide is a product of that decay or decay chain.

Carbon-14 is useful for relatively recent organic remains, often up to approximately 50,000 years. Potassium–argon can date suitable potassium-rich minerals after cooling or formation; uranium–lead is especially useful in zircon. Different methods constrain different events, and laboratory procedures depend on the material.

Try it: use the material-choice experiment. Can you radiocarbon-date a 150-million-year-old dinosaur? No: essentially no original carbon-14 could survive across that span, and fossil mineral replacement complicates the question. Instead, geologists often date surrounding volcanic layers or other age-constraining materials.

In the rock-layer experiment, the older layer is lower and the younger layer higher only when the sequence has not been overturned or disturbed. The fossil's age is then bracketed, not measured directly by the two ash ages.

Exponential decay, age inversion and isotope ratios

Let N(t) be the expected number of surviving parent atoms, N₀ the initial number, λ the decay constant (time⁻¹), and T½ the half-life. Independent decays with constant hazard satisfy:

dN/dt = −λN   ⇒   N(t) = N₀ e−λt
λ = ln(2) / T½   ;   t = ln(N₀ / N) / λ = −ln(f) / λ

Here f = N/N₀ is the surviving parent fraction. This is a convenient mathematical demonstration, but N₀ is generally not directly known in a real mineral. With a radiogenic daughter amount D*, branch fraction b, surviving parent P and suitable closed-system assumptions:

D* = b N₀ (1 − e−λt)   ;   t = ln(1 + D* / (bP)) / λ

Potassium–argon caveat: only roughly 10.7% of potassium-40 decays produce argon-40; the dominant branch produces calcium-40. Thus the argon daughter is not equal to all decayed parent atoms. The main graph correctly depicts all decay events, while the mineral disturbance exercise uses the explicit argon branch.

Uranium–lead caveat: uranium-238 reaches stable lead-206 through a multistep decay chain. The simple accumulated-daughter expression is an effective long-time approximation when the intermediate daughters are short-lived compared with the studied timescale; actual U–Pb calculations use full isotopic measurements and geological screening.

The age-estimator's interval follows from the chosen absolute error in the parent fraction. Because the relationship is logarithmic, symmetric uncertainty in f produces an asymmetric age interval. The example is not a statistical confidence interval, and the exercise excludes isotope fractionation, initial daughter correction, contamination, instrumental errors and uncertainty in decay constants.

From isotope ratios to defensible geological ages

The clock's zero-point is physical, not universal. Radiocarbon tracks cessation of biological carbon exchange, subject to reservoir and contamination effects. K–Ar may record cooling through effective argon retention rather than initial mineral crystallization. Zircon U–Pb commonly records crystallization, but later metamorphism, inherited cores or lead loss can alter the interpretation. Dodson's closure-temperature framework helps explain diffusion-controlled retention during cooling.

Initial daughter corrections and isochrons: many systems contain daughter nuclides when they form. Instead of assuming zero initial daughter, appropriately chosen co-genetic samples and isotope ratios can be used to fit an isochron. Agreement with model assumptions, mixing tests, and geological context matter; an apparent straight line is not automatically an age.

Dual U–Pb chronometers: uranium-238 → lead-206 and uranium-235 → lead-207 provide two independent decay constants. Concordant ages strengthen interpretation; discordance can indicate lead loss, inheritance or other open-system histories. Concordia/discordia analysis and error ellipses require actual isotope-ratio data and correlated uncertainties—our simple parent/daughter graph is not a concordia diagram.

Radiocarbon calibration: conventional radiocarbon ages are normally quoted in years BP (before AD 1950) using the conventional Libby half-life of 5,568 years and standard normalization. Calendar-age interpretation requires calibration datasets such as IntCal20, reflecting variation in past atmospheric ¹⁴C. A 5,730-year physical half-life exercise is neither a conventional BP determination nor a calibrated calendar date.

Uncertainty and model criticism: distinguish analytical precision from geological accuracy. Typical workflows incorporate standards, blanks, isotopic corrections, reference-material checks, propagated uncertainties and independent stratigraphic evidence. Examine the disturbance experiment: loss of radiogenic daughter gives a younger apparent age; additional daughter yields an older one in this simplified model. Real disturbances can be time-dependent, partial and spatially heterogeneous.

Research question: what corroborating evidence would make a billion-year mineral age trustworthy? Consider mineral textures, multiple isotope systems, geological setting, reproducible isotope measurements and agreement among grains.

READ THE MODEL CORRECTLY

What the simulation assumes

  • Parent atoms decay independently, each with a constant probability per unit time. The dot display is a seeded stochastic illustration; smooth curves are theoretical expected fractions.
  • For the age inversion, the initial parent fraction is known, decay constant is fixed, and the system remains closed. Real measurements require additional chemical and isotopic information.
  • Potassium-40 decay to argon-40 is a minority branch; the selected daughter in an actual K–Ar measurement is not interchangeable with total decays.
  • Uranium–lead is a decay chain; the simplified total-decayed graph does not explicitly simulate intermediate nuclides or concordia.
  • Isotope-system changes keep the slider's elapsed number of half-lives fixed rather than chronological age. Different clocks therefore show different calendar durations.
  • For uranium-238, times older than Earth are marked hypothetical. Older radiocarbon examples also exceed the usual practical measurement range.
  • All lab scenarios are invented pedagogical examples, not published geochronological measurements or laboratory reports.
