Dalton's atomic model: matter in tiny pieces

Why do substances combine in regular proportions? John Dalton's early nineteenth-century theory explained chemical composition by treating elements as collections of atoms that join in simple whole-number ratios. Experiment with this historical model, then discover what modern chemistry changed.

Dalton's postulates: achievements and corrections

Historical propositionModern understanding
Matter consists of atoms.Supported; atoms have internal structure.
Atoms are indivisible and indestructible.False universally: subatomic and nuclear processes exist. Atoms of each element are conserved in ordinary chemical reactions.
All atoms of an element have identical masses.Isotopes of one element differ in neutron number and mass.
Different elements have different atomic properties.Element identity is determined by proton number, not uniquely by mass.
Compounds combine atoms in characteristic whole-number ratios.Works for discrete molecular formulas and many stoichiometric solids; some solids are nonstoichiometric.

Dalton initially assumed a simple HO formula for water; the accepted molecular formula is H₂O. Historical chemical atomism was productive without every auxiliary assumption being correct.
